Insulin and Equipment

How I Kept Insulin Cool While Backpacking Southeast Asia

Five months, six countries, three FRIO wallets, many hot travel days and one hostel fridge incident in Vietnam that I would very much like to not repeat.

Published 31 July 2026 · Updated 31 July 2026 · 8 min read

Medical disclaimer: This is personal experience, not medical advice. I’m not giving insulin dosing advice or telling you how to store your medication. Speak to your diabetes team, pharmacist or healthcare professional before travel.

Why insulin storage worried me before travelling

Before travelling through Southeast Asia, insulin storage was one of the biggest things on my mind. Type 1 diabetes already comes with a travelling circus of supplies, and hot weather adds another layer of “please don’t ruin the medication keeping me alive.” Delightful.

I travelled for approximately five months through Thailand, Laos, Vietnam, Cambodia, Indonesia and Malaysia. That meant buses, hostels, islands, city heat, long journeys and plenty of moments where I had to make a practical decision rather than a perfect one.

What equipment I packed

For cooling, I carried two extra-large FRIO wallets and one duo pen wallet. The idea was simple: keep insulin protected during hot days and use accommodation fridges carefully when they were available.

Using FRIO wallets in Southeast Asian heat

The FRIO wallets were useful because they did not rely on electricity. That mattered on long travel days, bus journeys and when I didn’t fully trust the storage situation. They gave me a practical backup when I was moving between places or didn’t have immediate fridge access.

They were not magic. I still had to think about where the insulin was sitting, avoid leaving it in direct sun, and keep checking my kit rather than assuming everything was fine because it was inside a wallet.

Hostels, buses and long travel days

Travel days were the times I had to be most switched on. If I was moving for hours, I wanted insulin accessible, protected and not buried somewhere it could overheat. Hostels could be helpful, but they also introduced a different risk: shared fridges, uncertain temperatures and staff who may not understand how sensitive insulin can be.

The mistake that froze my insulin in Vietnam

During the Hà Giang Loop in Vietnam, my insulin was accidentally frozen in a hostel refrigerator. That was the kind of situation I had worried about before leaving, and then, helpfully, it happened in real life.

The lesson was not “never use fridges.” The lesson was: don’t assume a fridge is safe just because it is cold. Some fridges freeze items at the back or near the cooling element. If I use one, I want the insulin placed carefully, clearly labelled, checked, and ideally not pushed into the coldest part.

What I would do differently

  • Ask more clearly where medication will be stored and whether the fridge has frozen items before.
  • Use a labelled container or bag so staff understand it is medical equipment.
  • Avoid the back wall or freezer-adjacent sections of shared fridges.
  • Check insulin sooner rather than leaving it overnight and hoping for the best.
  • Keep more insulin in cooling wallets during uncertain storage situations.

My practical insulin-storage checklist

  • Carry cooling equipment that works without electricity.
  • Keep insulin out of direct sun and hot vehicles where possible.
  • Check fridge temperature risk before using hostel or hotel fridges.
  • Keep medical documents and prescriptions accessible.
  • Pack backup supplies separately in case one bag goes missing.
  • Speak to your own healthcare team before travelling.

Frequently asked questions

Can FRIO wallets guarantee insulin safety?

No. They can be useful cooling tools, but no product guarantees insulin safety in every condition. Always follow your medication guidance and professional advice.

Should insulin go in hostel fridges?

Sometimes it may be useful, but shared fridges need caution. My Vietnam mistake taught me to ask questions, avoid freezing zones and check regularly.

Did travelling with insulin get easier?

Yes, but not because it became effortless. It got easier because I built routines and stopped treating every decision like a full-blown crisis meeting.
