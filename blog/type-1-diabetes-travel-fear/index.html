What Travelling With Type 1 Diabetes Taught Me About Fear

By OlliePersonal essay6 minute read

Travelling with Type 1 diabetes did not teach me to be fearless. “Fearless” is mostly a word people use after editing out the part where they panicked in a hostel bathroom.

I still worry about insulin temperatures, nighttime hypos, broken sensors, food and being far from medical help. The difference is that I no longer treat fear as proof that I should stay home.

Fear often disguises itself as responsible planning

Before my trip, anxious thoughts sounded completely sensible:

What if my insulin overheats? What if it freezes? What if airport security takes my supplies? What if my glucose drops on a twelve-hour bus?

Every question contained a real possibility, which made it easy to research forever. Preparation was necessary. Eventually, though, I had to accept there was no packing list capable of guaranteeing that nothing would go wrong. Slightly awkward for somebody promoting a packing list, but true.

Problems changed what confidence meant

Across Southeast Asia, things did go wrong. I had stomach illnesses, unpredictable journeys and a hostel freezer incident involving five months of insulin.

None of that proved I was foolish to travel. It taught me that confidence is not believing a trip will be smooth. It is trusting that when something changes, you can stop, ask for help and make the next decision.

Responsibility and fear are not the same thing

Some worries require action. If insulin has reached an unsafe temperature, my glucose is behaving strangely or I cannot treat a hypo, that is a practical problem.

The useful question is whether fear is pointing towards a specific action or demanding complete certainty. I can respond to the first. The second is unavailable to every traveller, diabetic or otherwise.

Diabetes made me pay attention

Type 1 demands constant awareness. That can be exhausting, especially somewhere unfamiliar, but it also made me resourceful. I notice where my supplies are, how long a journey lasts, where I can find food and who nearby should know about my condition.

Those habits do not remove spontaneity. They build enough structure for spontaneity to happen without becoming stupidity.

I have not defeated fear. I have collected evidence that I can act while feeling it. Every border crossing, hostel and unfamiliar meal adds another piece. Diabetes remains part of every journey, but it does not get to write the entire story.

A personal reflection, not medical advice. Good preparation and support matter. Your diabetes team can help you plan around your own needs before travelling.
