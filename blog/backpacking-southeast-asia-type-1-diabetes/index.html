What Backpacking Southeast Asia With Type 1 Diabetes Actually Looked Like

It was not effortless. It was admin-heavy, occasionally stressful, often brilliant and slowly less terrifying than it looked from the sofa before I left.

Published 31 July 2026 · Updated 31 July 2026 · 9 min read

Medical disclaimer: This article is personal experience, not medical advice. Speak to your own healthcare team before travelling with Type 1 diabetes, severe allergies or prescription medication.

Fear before leaving

Before leaving, the biggest fear was not just “what if something goes wrong?” It was the number of ways something could go wrong: insulin storage, supplies, food, allergies, heat, language barriers and long travel days. Very relaxing. Basically a spa break, but with more paperwork.

Carrying months of medical supplies

Backpacking with Type 1 diabetes meant carrying far more than clothes and a phone charger. Supplies had to be organised, protected and split sensibly. I had to think ahead because replacing medical kit abroad is not the same as popping to the corner shop.

Thailand and Laos

Thailand and Laos were part of learning the rhythm of travelling with diabetes: checking kit, thinking about food, managing heat and not letting the admin take over the whole trip. Editor note: add one specific Thailand or Laos memory if Ollie wants a more personal opening here.

The slow boat journey

The slow boat journey was the kind of travel day that makes preparation matter. Long journeys are not just about boredom; they affect meals, access to supplies, hypo treatments, hydration and where your insulin is sitting for hours.

Vietnam and the frozen-insulin incident

Vietnam included the Hà Giang Loop, which was incredible, but also the place where insulin was accidentally frozen in a hostel refrigerator. It was a very clear reminder that “cold” is not automatically “safe” and that shared accommodation needs extra care when medication is involved.

Cambodia and Action4Diabetes

Cambodia included volunteering with Action4Diabetes. That added another layer to the trip: meeting people, seeing the wider Type 1 community and connecting the travel story to something bigger than just moving from place to place.

Indonesia, jungle trekking and island travel

Indonesia brought more adventure: jungle trekking, island travel and the practical challenge of keeping routines stable when the setting is anything but. This is where travel content becomes useful for other people — not just showing the view, but showing how the experience can feel possible.

What became easier

Over time, the checks became routines. I got quicker at knowing what mattered, what to ask, where to keep supplies and when to be cautious. Confidence came from repetition, not from suddenly becoming a motivational poster.

What remained difficult

Food uncertainty, heat, carrying supplies and explaining medical needs did not disappear. Some days still required more energy than I wanted to spend. The point is not that travel became effortless; it became manageable.

What I learned

I learned that travel with Type 1 diabetes and severe allergies needs preparation, honesty and backup plans. I also learned that people are actively looking for this kind of information. The 300+ messages asking for advice made it obvious: anxious travellers do not just need inspiration. They need practical reassurance.
